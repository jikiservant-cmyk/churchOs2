1. **They already have a multi-location structure.** "TJM Mengo and all locations," "all our oikoses" — they

2. **Their cell model needs exactly what you sell.** Oikos = household cell, meeting Wednesday 6pm. Oikos

3. **They have a follow-up problem, and it is measurable.** 997 salvations in a three-day Iganga campaign.

4. **They are young, fast, and un-bureaucratic.** Founder-led, medical-student DNA, no procurement committee.

5. **They are on your doorstep.** **TJM Makerere** and **TJM Mengo** are both in Kampala. You do not need to

### How to reach them (in order)

1. **Walk into TJM Makerere or TJM Mengo on a Sunday.** You are in Kampala; they are campus churches. Ask
   for the **location pastor**, not the founder.
2. **Go to Oikos Time, Wednesday 6pm** — smaller, and the oikos leaders are the ones who feel the pain of
   paper attendance lists.
3. **YouTube: comment on a live stream and DM the channel** @TJMAreteChurches. They stream daily, so a
   message lands in front of a media team that is actively working.
4. **Ask about the Church Growth Conference (CGC)** — they run it for their own leaders and guests. A
   training slot there is the equivalent of the COPE youth-desk play.
5. **Do not start at Rev. Nkuusi.** He is the founder of a multi-country movement; his inbox is not the door.

**What to say (WhatsApp / DM):**
> "Praise God. I'm [name], a Kampala developer. I watched the Iganga campaign — 997 salvations. I've built a

**What not to say:** don't call it "church management software" (sounds bureaucratic to a movement), don't
compare them to a denomination, and don't mention money in the first message. Their language is *souls,
oikos, follow-up, locations* — use those words.

## 1b. The other thing called "The Jesus Movement" (so you don't mix them up)

The name also belongs to a specific American revival, and knowing it is still useful because its structure is
the one TJM has copied.

| | |
|---|---|
| **What** | A youth-led revival among American hippies and students, roughly **1967 to the mid-1970s**, fading by the 1980s |
| **Where** | California — Calvary Chapel (Costa Mesa), communes, coffee houses, beach baptisms |
| **How it spread** | Not through denominations. Through **music, coffee houses, communes and campus fellowships** |
| **What it produced** | Organisations, not churches: Calvary Chapel, the Vineyard, Maranatha, Jews for Jesus, and the contemporary Christian music industry |
| **Signature event** | **Explo '72** — Campus Crusade for Christ, Cotton Bowl, Dallas, ~80,000 students, June 1972. Called "the Christian Woodstock" |

**The lesson:** that movement didn't build a denomination. It built **student networks and event brands**,
then let the churches absorb the converts. TJM is doing the same thing in Uganda in real time — which is
exactly why its follow-up gap is your opening.

## 2. Uganda's own version of that story

The local equivalent of the Jesus Movement isn't American — it's the **East African Revival (Balokole)**,
which reached Uganda from Gahini (Rwanda) in the early 1930s and turned "being saved" into a mass youth
phenomenon inside the Anglican and Pentecostal churches.

The organisational chain that came out of it:

```
Balokole revival (1930s)  →  Makerere University Christian Union (1962)
                          →  Christian Unions spread to every institution
                          →  FOCUS, the Fellowship of Christian Unions (1972)
                          →  all the campus ministries below
                          →  and, in the 2020s, TJM / Arete Churches — a CU-shaped
                             movement that became churches
```

**Makerere CU was founded in 1962** by Daniel Kyanda and Bishop Michael Ssenyimba (later Vice-Chancellor of
Ndejje University), and it is the reason every other CU in the country exists. In 2002 it was reported as
the largest fellowship at the university with **over 2,000 students**, organised into **Hall, College and
Hostel fellowships**. It celebrated 60 years in 2022.

**This is the single most important structural fact in this document:** campus fellowships are organised
*by hall and hostel*, which means each one is already subdivided into small groups with a leader. You are
not pitching to a crowd. You are pitching to ~10 named leaders per campus.

### The two sub-bodies inside FOCUS that matter to you

- **FATT — FOCUS Associate Trainers' Team** (since 1994): recent graduates who travel and train CU leaders.
  Central Region alone has the biggest and most numerous CUs.
- **STEM — Short Term Experience in Ministry**: a **one-year programme for fresh graduates** who work
  directly with a CU. These are 22–25 year olds with a title, a mandate and no salary security — the exact
  profile of your "young church planter" buyer.
- **FOCUS Associates**: 5,000+ graduates, organised in town branches, meeting over **"Christian Perspectives"
  breakfasts** (running since 2017, ~50 per sitting, with an Associates WhatsApp group). **Associates are
  the ones with money and churches.** An Associates breakfast is the single best room in this document.

## 5. The complication you must know before you walk onto a campus

A 2022 research paper, *Making Phaneroo Manifest* (Veracity Fount), documents real conflict inside
**Makerere and MUBS Christian Unions** between FOCUS-aligned CU leadership and students who were also
Phaneroo members — disputes over doctrine, discipline and control of the fellowship, serious enough to
become a university administrative matter.

**What that means for you:**

1. **Do not pick a side, ever.** The moment you're "the Phaneroo guy" or "the FOCUS guy," you lose half the
   campus.
2. **Sell records, not theology.** Your pitch is attendance, member registers, SMS. It works for a CU of
   2,000 and for a 30-member hall fellowship, and it is theologically neutral by design.
3. **Expect gatekeepers.** FOCUS staff are protective of CUs — correctly, given the above. Go through the
   **General Secretary or the regional office**, not around them, and never recruit a CU into a church.

## 6. Three ways in, ranked

**🥇 1. A FOCUS Associates breakfast (or the Associates WhatsApp group)**
Graduates, professionals, church planters. Ask the FOCUS office for the date of the next Christian
Perspectives breakfast in Kampala and for permission to attend. Ten minutes there beats 100 DMs.

**🥈 2. The CU handover season — right now**
It is **mid-November**: CU executives are handing over and incoming committees are being inducted for the
new academic year. This is the cheapest possible moment to meet next year's leaders, because nobody is
competing for their attention. Ask each campus for the **incoming chairperson's** number, not the outgoing
one.

**🥉 3. The professional student bodies — UCMF first**
Medical students are the most organised students in the country, they have a published executive list with
phone numbers, they are affiliated to FOCUS, and they become junior doctors with income in 12 months. Start
with the UCMF student chapter, not the national office.

## 8. Honest limits

- **They can't pay.** CUs and FOCUS are donor-funded. Treat every campus contact as a route to a *church*,
  not as a customer. Never quote a price to a student.
- **The calendar is brutal.** CU life peaks August–November and February–May. Late November is handover and
  exams; December–January and June–July are dead. If a CU says "after exams," that means February.
- **Verify before you travel.** Uganda 4 Jesus and similar rally brands come and go; check they still exist
  before spending a boda fare.
- **One CU is not a market.** The prize is the FOCUS office and the Associates list, not any single campus.
